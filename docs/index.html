How much does the next gigawatt of batteries earn in Poland?

Batteries earn from the spread between cheap and expensive hours, and every battery that charges at noon and discharges in the evening narrows that spread for all the others. This page replays the 2025 day-ahead prices (RCE) with a fleet of 0-10 GW of batteries that moves prices along an empirical supply curve fitted on public PSE data, and shows what is left for each MW. It illustrates the mechanism; it is not a price forecast.

Generated 2026-10-22 07:49 UTC from PSE data obtained 2026-10-22. Price impact scale 0.84 (calibrated out of sample). Code and method

Revenue per MW

Arbitrage revenue net of a 30 PLN/MWh degradation cost, per MW of power and year. Dashed: marginal revenue of the whole fleet, what one more MW adds once its effect on the incumbents is counted. The shaded band (2 h) spans impact scales 0.5 to 1.0.

Revenue of the whole fleet

Per-MW revenue times fleet power. Where the curve flattens, more batteries add capacity but no revenue to the fleet as a whole.

Average day

Mean price by local time of day, as observed and after the fleet's impact.

What the fleet does

Mean net charging of the fleet by local time of day (positive = charging, raises the load; negative = discharging).

Daily spread

Mean daily maximum minus minimum price, and the mean evening (17-21) minus midday (10-15) price difference.

Negative prices

Clock hours per year with a negative average price. Charging lifts the cheapest hours first.

The supply curve behind it

Price against residual load (demand minus PV and wind) in a winter and a summer month at 03:00, 13:00 and 19:00: binned observations and the fitted monotone curve of that month and hour. A battery moves the load left (discharging) or right (charging) along the curve.

Is the curve right? Out-of-sample validation

Day-to-day change: price difference between the same quarter-hour on two consecutive days, against the difference the curve predicts; the hour-of-day pattern cancels, so this tests the slope. Response ratio: OLS slope of actual on predicted changes for load moves of at least 2 GW (1 = right on average, below 1 = the curve overstates the response). Comparison with a price taker

At 0 GW the model reproduces the price-taker backtest of bess-arbitrage-pl exactly (same battery parameters: 87% round-trip efficiency, SoC 5-95%, at most 1.5 cycles per day, 30 PLN/MWh degradation cost). That backtest is the revenue of the first MW; this page asks what is left for the next ones.

Limitations

  • No model of the coupled European market: imports and exports react to the fleet only as much as they did historically along the fitted curve. Batteries abroad, new interconnectors and flow-based coupling are not modelled.
  • Nothing else reacts: the PV, wind, demand, conventional fleet, pumped storage and their bids stay as observed in 2025. In reality curtailment at negative prices, demand response and plant retirements change the curve.
  • Day-ahead energy arbitrage only, with perfect foresight per day; no balancing, reserves, capacity market, intraday trading or grid fees. The fleet is one aggregate of identical batteries in a competitive market.
  • The supply curve is fitted on 2024-2026 data with one price-impact scale for all seasons; the response ratio is higher in winter and lower in spring.
